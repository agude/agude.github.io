# Review Plan

## Status

- **Lead-in: drafted.** Hook is publication-vs-composition ("published after
  Neuromancer and Count Zero, but most of the stories were written before,
  and it shows in two ways" --- now three). Structure: 1. prose, 2. ideas
  reused, 3. the one pattern that *doesn't* survive.
- **Gernsback: drafted**, still marked for polish.
- **Everything else: story sections empty.**

## The frame

**Not used in the lead.** The publication-vs-composition hook won instead ---
it earns the argument in one sentence rather than asserting an image. The
gomi frame below is still unplaced; best remaining home is The Winter Market,
where it can land on the collection sideways.

The trilogy reviews all trace influence *outward* (Gibson -> Stephenson ->
The Matrix -> Shadowrun). This one traces it *inward*: what did Gibson give
to himself?

**Best available image: this collection is Gibson's own gomi.** The Mona Lisa
Overdrive review already built the vocabulary --- gomi as junk that carries
memory, Slick's kinetic sculptures from the Jersey Solitude, the box-maker
sorting refuse into art, Tessier-Ashpool hoarding a cut-up past. Burning
Chrome *is* the parts bin for the trilogy. And "The Winter Market" is
literally about a junk artist (Rubin, *gomi no sensei*) who makes celebrated
art out of discarded parts --- which is exactly what Gibson did to these ten
stories. The collection performs its own thesis.

This also closes the loop from the end of the Mona Lisa Overdrive review
("looking forward to Burning Chrome, the collection where the Sprawl began").

## Lead-in material: prose, ideas, and the pattern that dies

Three beats. 1 and 3 rhyme (things he outgrew); 2 is the middle (the thing
he kept).

### Prose --- you can watch him learn

**Drafted.** Key correction made during drafting: the blanket claim ("these
early works are much heavier") is false for New Rose Hotel and Burning
Chrome, which are as stripped as anything he wrote, and the notes above call
Hinterlands "way better." Fixed by making it a *trajectory* --- heavy early,
"by New Rose Hotel his prose is stripped down and lean."

**Collaboration stat deliberately not used here.** The three co-written
stories being the three lowest-rated (solo avg 4.0 vs. co-written 2.7) is
evidence that collaboration dilutes him --- it says nothing about early prose
being heavier. Different claim. And the only real evidence for a prose claim
is quoted prose, which is too much freight for this paragraph. Left as
per-story "Co-written with X" notes.

### Ideas --- the prototype map

**Only three stories are canonically Sprawl** --- Johnny Mnemonic, New Rose
Hotel, Burning Chrome --- via shared characters or geography (Molly, the
Finn, Chiba City). Proto-Sprawl but not canon: Fragments of a Hologram Rose
(ASP prefigures simstim, no connective tissue), Dogfight (reads Sprawl-ish,
nothing ties it), The Winter Market (Vancouver; adjacent, and this one gets
argued about --- verify against the text before leaning on it).

Sharpens the claim: only three of ten are *set* in the Sprawl, but six build
it. "These stories mostly aren't set in the Sprawl. They're the Sprawl being
invented." Also justifies the `book_number: 0` shelving --- the collection
belongs to the series as origin, not installment.

| Story | Prototype for |
|---|---|
| Johnny Mnemonic | Molly, the Finn, the Yakuza, wetware storage --- straight into Neuromancer |
| Fragments of a Hologram Rose | ASP -> simstim; body-image dysphoria in the recordings |
| New Rose Hotel | Count Zero's entire Turner plot: defection, extraction, the woman who burns it down |
| The Winter Market | Mona Lisa Overdrive: Rubin->Slick, Lise->Angie, the upload->the Aleph |
| Burning Chrome | cyberspace itself, the Finn, black ice, Rikki->Mona |
| Dogfight | Riviera's holographic projection; drugged-up flyers |

**Hinterlands belongs on this list too, thematically.** Note above: "language
of the shaman and the kabbalist and the mystic survives. Like Count Zero."
That is exactly the Count Zero review's thesis --- humans metabolize
incomprehensible power through religion --- arriving five years early, with
the Loa swapped for cosmic dread. It has the trilogy's theme without the
trilogy's furniture.

Counterpoint worth stating: **Hinterlands is the one great idea Gibson never
went back to.** Everything else here got cannibalized. The best story in the
collection is the one that wasn't useful to him.

### The pattern that dies --- women

**Beat 3 of the lead. Drafted.** The collection's real structural obsession,
more consistent than the cyberspace material: the narrator is a man watching
a woman with more will than he has. She makes the trade, she gets what she
wants, she leaves him behind. The men are witnesses.

Used in the lead:

- Jack loses Rikki to the eyes she sold herself to buy (Burning Chrome)
- Sandii burns down Fox *and* the narrator (New Rose Hotel) --- **naming
  trap:** the narrator is unnamed and Fox is his partner; Fox dies but it's
  the narrator who ends up in the coffin hotel. Don't write "Fox loses
  everything to Sandii."
- Casey loses Lise to the machine she uploads into (The Winter Market)

Held back for their own sections:

- Parker sifts his ex's ASP recordings (Fragments of a Hologram Rose)
- The Belonging Kind opens on a woman the narrator can't keep up with
- Lise was only an artist *because* the exoskeleton trapped her

Payoff: by the novels it's gone --- Molly, Marly, Angela, Mona, Kumiko all
get chapters of their own. A developmental claim, not a gripe. If airtightness
matters, "by the novels he's mostly done with it" --- Molly in Neuromancer is
still filtered through Case a fair amount.

## Themes to distribute into story sections (keep the lead small)

### The Gernsback Continuum --- DRAFTED

Structure that shipped: "doesn't write horror but much of what he writes is
horrifying" as the setup for normal-Gibson mode, then three inversions.

1. Usually looks at a possible future; here he looks back at one that never
   arrived.
2. Horror is usually in the details (puppet shops, custom-tailored cancers),
   so commonplace the prose hardly has time for them. Here nothing is grim at
   all --- the seamlessness *is* the threat. Ties to the Hitler Youth note:
   1930s streamline futurism and fascist monumentalism share an impulse
   toward the frictionless.
3. Squalor is usually the disease. Here it's the cure --- porn, bad
   television, and the ugly real world are what break the spell.

Then the verdict: clunky prose, characters don't matter, all *idea*. Justifies
the 3, and applies the same criterion as the Neuromancer review ("big idea
sci-fi where the story and characters are just there").

Still open:

- **"It could be worse. It could be perfect."** Almost certainly the story's
  closing beat but the exact wording is unverified --- check the text before
  quoting. Left out of the draft deliberately; ending on the verdict serves
  the rating better than ending on the quote.
- Cut: the **anti-gomi** angle (the Gernsback future has no junk, nothing
  repurposed, no street --- which is why it's dead). It's really a restatement
  of inversion 2 and costs a paragraph to set up. Available if the gomi frame
  ever needs a second pole.

### Body as capital --- put in Johnny Mnemonic

Continuous with the Neuromancer review's "Power: What It Costs You," and more
pointed here because there's no plot to soften it. Johnny rents out his
skull. Rikki sells herself at the House of Blue Lights for eyes. Lise trades
her whole body for the upload. Dogfight's pilots dose for reflexes. The
puppet shop --- already linked to Dollhouse in the Neuromancer review ---
*originates here*, not in Neuromancer.

"You can hardly tell they're human anymore" (Johnny Mnemonic) is the same
observation as Marly meeting Virek: *the exceedingly rich were no longer even
remotely human.* Top and bottom of the ladder, same transformation, different
price.

### Corporations as a life form --- put in New Rose Hotel

The note above is the sharpest thing in the file and should survive nearly
verbatim: *if an alien came to Earth, who would it identify as the dominant
life form? Zaibatsus. And their lifeblood is information, not people.*

Cleanest statement of the idea anywhere in Gibson. Neuromancer review called
it "DNA coded in silicon"; Count Zero review called it Virek's autonomous,
self-warring wealth. Ties to Accelerando (already made twice). Maas as the
small fast predator killing the big slow one is the part that has aged into
the present tense.

### Wonder and dread --- Hinterlands section (mostly drafted already)

The half-remembered Banks story is **"Cleaning Up" from The State of the
Art** --- aliens' trash falls to Earth, humans read it as gifts. This exact
pairing was already made in the Valuable Humans review, linking it to "A
Powerful Culture." Three-way lineage available: Roadside Picnic is the
origin, Cleaning Up is the comedy, Hinterlands is the horror. Captures for
both already exist in the file.

### Reggae / Afro-Caribbean religion --- put in Red Star, Winter Orbit

"So many of these books mention reggae" is the Rastafarian/dub thread that
becomes Zion cluster in Neuromancer and Vodou in Count Zero. Gibson reaches
for Afro-Caribbean religion and music as the counterweight to zaibatsu
technology --- the "parallel system," in the Count Zero review's phrasing.
One sentence noting it's a habit, not an accident.

### Closing line

"The street finds its own uses for things" is from the title story.

Do **not** call it his most-quoted line --- that's "the future is already
here, it's just not evenly distributed" (and the most-quoted *prose* is the
dead-channel sky). But "not evenly distributed" is a spoken interview remark
that Gibson himself can't source; it has no home in the fiction. "The street
finds its own uses for things" does, and the home is this book. So the true
claim is: it's the most durable idea in the collection, the one that outgrew
its container --- which is the same argument the review makes about
everything else here.

## Factual corrections to the notes above

- **Chrome is human**, not an AI. A fixer/launderer connected to the mob who
  fronts the House of Blue Lights. The black ice and tailored cancers are
  what she *buys*, not what she is. Matters because the drafted "puppet
  shops, fast-growing customer-tailored cancers" line leans on her.
- **Bobby Quine != Bobby Newmark.** Two different Bobbys, twelve years apart
  in the timeline. The Finn is genuinely the same Finn.
- **Jack's arm and Screaming Fist are the same war.** The hang-glide into
  Kiev and Corto's raid belong to one conflict; Gibson kept the backstory
  consistent from 1982 through 1984. Good evidence for "these are drafts of
  the same world."
- **The Gernsback Continuum references nothing in particular.** Hugo
  Gernsback edited *Amazing Stories*; the target is 1930s streamline-moderne
  pulp futurism (Frank R. Paul's airbrushed cities), not a specific novel ---
  not Bradbury. "Semiotic ghosts" is Gibson's own coinage. The Probability
  Broach instinct is right on the alternate-America axis, but that book is
  wish-fulfillment about a *better* America and Gibson's is about a
  fascist-adjacent one --- which is why he goes straight to the Hitler Youth
  comparison.
